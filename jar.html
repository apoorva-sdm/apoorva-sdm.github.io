OVERVIEW

Jar primarily caters to first-time investors, mostly in Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities. The primary customer acquisition channel is advertisement and users invest small amounts in digital gold. Each study began with a joint assumption from the user research and product team and the hypothesis was tested against the app usage of users. The research studies followed users across the whole user lifecycle over a course of 5 months.

Key Findings

Onboarding Drop-Off

Onboarding

New users who reach the Auto-Pay step but do not complete it are likely to have had no prior intent to save daily. For these users, drop-off at this step is driven by low intent and would persist regardless of how clearly the product is explained.

Sample size: 103

Users installing the app but were not setting up Auto-pay

78% of user acquisition is through ad channel

28% of users did not understand the app during onboarding

Only 3% of users did not have an intention to save

Jar ReadyCash

Lending: Usability

Users with past loan experience can move through the Ready Cash flow quickly, because speed and pre-filled Jar details reduce effort. The main usability barriers are transparency and trust at decision points, not about whether people can complete the tasks.

Sample size: 10 users

Remote usability testing, think-aloud, stage-by-stage discovery

6 of 10 users considered interest rate as the deciding factor

4 of 10 users needed more time on critical screens

4 of 10 of users praise the 15-min loan disbursal

Credit & Lending

Lending: Discovery

Users’ familiarity with credit lines is limited to a small, higher-income, urban segment. For most users, a credit line would be a new concept that needs explaining and trust-building first. Their need for money is real, but today it is met through family, friends, salary advances and familiar loan formats.

Sample size: 100 users

75 Jar users + 25 Non-Jar users

Only 7% of users earning above ₹30,000/per month in white-collar jobs knew what a credit line is

49% of users who have borrowed a loan wanted to borrow again

27% of non-loan holders possessed a credit card

GoldX Drop-off

Lending

Users who drop off at the GoldX Summary or Success screen are likely to be casual explorers or users who never understood the feature, not users who weighed up the offer and rejected it.

Sample size: 50 users

Users who dropped-off at Summary or Success screens

52% of users explored GoldX without intent

64% of users had no real understanding of the feature

56% of users were interested in leasing in the future

Understanding GoldX Users

Lending

Users who lease gold on GoldX are likely to understand the concept of leasing and that they will earn something, but not how those earnings are calculated and paid. The decision to lease therefore rests on trust in the feature and the expectation of a return.

Sample size: 71 users

Cohort 1 users who leased gold Cohort 1.1 users who topped up their savings to lease

70% of users in Cohort 1 & 62% of users in Cohort 1.1 perceived their lease to be in grams not rupees

28% of users understood how earnings would work

88% of users recalled the feature quickly

Understanding Loyal Users

Retention

Loyal users who have transacted continuously for ten months are likely to keep saving because they want to build holdings in digital gold. For these users, gold is the main motivation for staying, ahead of the convenience of saving small amounts.

Sample size: 100 users

Users using Jar for >10 months

36% of users use Jar as a means of short-term savings

23% of users trusted Jar post a first withdrawal of money

Only 4% of users intended to invest in gold since app installation

Motivations behind using Weekly Magic Feature

Engagement

Users who complete the Weekly Magic challenge for two weeks and then drop out are likely to do so because they lose track of what each week requires, not because they lack motivation. Consistent users stay in because they know the current challenge’s details and how to complete it.

Sample size: 80 users

Consistent cohort with 3-week streak Inconsistent cohort with 2-week drop-off

41% of users (inclusive of both cohorts) play to save

33% of users from the inconsistent cohort drop out because they do not open the app in time to finish the challenge

DEEP-DIVE INTO Daily Savings drop-off during onboarding RESEARCH STUDY

New users who install Jar go through onboarding, but a share of them stop at the Auto-Pay step, where daily savings is set up. Auto-Pay is what turns a first visit into a recurring savings habit, therefore drop-off at this step affects whether a new user becomes an active saver at all. This study was chosen as the lead study because it addresses a critical point in the funnel: the step between onboarding and a first recurring saving. The study set out to understand why users who reached the Auto-Pay step did not complete it. It was run with new users who reached that step and left, using live data to identify and interview them.

Insights

Intent to save exists. The product isn’t turning it into a plan.

Only 3% had no intention to save, and only ~13% could say what they were saving for, even though onboarding asks for a goal. Most users have a general wish to save but no goal that registers, & the goal step doesn’t give them a reason to set up a daily commitment rejecting the initial hypothesis.

Drop-off starts with a mismatch between the ad and the app, before onboarding begins.

78% of users installed Jar through ads but 47% took it for a savings app. 24% thought it was a gaming app, 9% a money-making app, and about 27% were expecting something else entirely.

Outcome

Re-design of the onboarding screens to:

Correct misperception of the product and explain what Jar is

Strengthen goal setting by enabling clearer and more engaging goal commitment step

CORE FINDINGS FROM STUDIES

App perception driving drop-off rates

Auto-pay vs manual pay dilemma

Every consistent user with auto-pay has made top-up manual payments to complete the Weekly Magic Challenge although data shows an inverse
